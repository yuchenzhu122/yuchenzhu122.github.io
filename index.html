Job Market Paper

Predicting College Major Switching with Machine Learning

Machine LearningXGBoostSHAPPrediction Policy

Students in U.S. colleges can change their majors as they learn about their academic strengths and fit with different fields, but adjusting late is costly. Using administrative data, I examine what types of information predict major switching. Information available before college predicts switching only weakly. Prediction improves once students enroll and their records accumulate. Performance in a major’s core courses is key, predicting even switching that occurs several terms after the courses are taken. Routinely collected academic records can therefore identify switching risk well before the switch takes place, leaving time for advising to respond. At the same advising capacity in each term, a machine learning model identifies 18 percent more next-term switchers than the university's current advising system.

Migration Responses to Targeted K–12 Funding: Evidence from California’s Local Control Funding Formula Draft (PDF)

MIGRATEZillow ZHVIEvent StudyDifference-in-Differences

Targeted reforms in school finance boost resources in disadvantaged school districts. Whether this reduces socioeconomic stratification depends on how families respond and how elastic local housing supply is. We study this question using California’s Local Control Funding Formula and origin-to-destination migration data that track residential flows across districts. We compare districts by baseline exposure to the formula. Five years after the reform, home values in more exposed districts were still 7 to 14% higher than in less exposed districts. Net in-migration increased in several post-reform years, but the effects were small and student composition changed little. The reform was reflected mainly in housing prices, with limited evidence of reduced residential stratification.

The Academic Effects of Performance Requirements for Staying in College Majors

with Robert Ainsworth, Thomas Knight, and Macy McClaren

Regression DiscontinuityHigher EducationMajor Choice

Policies that restrict access to college majors have become increasingly common in popular majors, yet their effects remain poorly understood. Using a regression discontinuity design based on GPA cutoffs together with data on around 30 in-demand majors from a large U.S. research university, we estimate the causal impacts of a policy where students who are in a major must meet term-specific performance requirements; otherwise, they go on probation and may be forced to switch majors. We find that going on this type of probation: (i) reduces a student’s probability of graduating in their initial major by 14 pp (29%), (ii) decreases the probability of graduating at all by 3 pp (3%) and the probability of graduating in 5 and 6 years by 4 pp (5% and 4%, respectively), (iii) pushes students into majors with weaker labor market prospects, and (iv) makes students take slightly easier courses. Exploiting across-major variation in the magnitude of impacts on major-switching, we show that treatment effects are only partially explained by switching costs, suggesting that other factors such as discouragement also play an important role.
